> **TL;DR** — At 20:05 UTC on October 29, 2026, funds began leaving Binance's hot wallets. In about 20 minutes, roughly \$6.3 billion was gone across 11 chains. It is the largest crypto theft on record. Binance said it found **no evidence of a private-key leak**. The attacker didn't steal the keys. They fed their own transactions into **the system that signs with those keys**. On Ethereum, an EIP-7702 delegation turned a hot wallet into a "sweeper" for 3 hours and 49 minutes.

## 1. What happened — the officially confirmed scope

Binance's official notice confirms the following.

- It started with a compromised **DevOps engineer's laptop**. Through it, the attacker reached an internal **service container**.
- From that container, the attacker submitted unauthorized withdrawal transactions.
- There is no evidence that any wallet private key leaked.
- Losses are about \$6.3 billion. About \$3 billion has been frozen.
- Binance will cover all losses from the SAFU fund and company funds.

Binance later said it had found **"additional breach points"** beyond the laptop. Co-CEO Richard Teng described the EIP-7702 delegation this way: it **"did not pass through our normal approval controls."**

> **Not yet public** — How the laptop was first infected is still unknown. So are the additional breach points, and the authority the container held over the signing system.

## 3. The keys were safe — the signing-delegation paradox at exchange scale

A phone's Secure Enclave never lets a wallet key out. But what if malware *asks it to sign a transaction of its choosing*? The secure element signs it anyway. The key stays safe while the funds are drained — the [signing-delegation paradox](/en/posts/mobile-wallet-key-security).

The Binance incident is that paradox at exchange scale. Side by side:

| Layer | Mobile wallet | Binance hot wallet |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Key custody | Secure Enclave / StrongBox | HSM or MPC signing system (setup undisclosed) |
| Who requests signatures | Wallet app | Internal service container |
| What the attacker controlled | Malware deceiving the app or the user | Engineer laptop → container |
| Result | Key intact, funds stolen | Key intact, funds stolen on 11 chains |

<SigningInfraBoundary locale="en" />

Blockaid's CEO called it **"key misuse, not key theft."** The wallets on the other 10 chains all use different key types and signing schemes. Yet within about 30 minutes, every one of them sent funds via *validly signed ordinary transactions*. Binance's second Ethereum hot wallet also sent \$174 million in nine ordinary transactions, with no delegation involved. Phishing can explain Ethereum alone. So TRM and Elliptic both conclude the attacker could **issue commands directly to the hot-wallet signing infrastructure**.

> **Key insight** — "No private key was leaked" does not mean "safe." The security boundary does not stop at **where the key lives**. It must cover **every path that can request a signature**.

## 4. The EIP-7702 model — delegation designators and the nonce rule

EIP-7702 lets an EOA hand its code over to a smart contract. It does so through an authorization the EOA signs.

- An authorization is a `(chain_id, address, nonce, y_parity, r, s)` tuple. It is submitted inside the `authorization_list` of a type-4 transaction.
- Once applied, a **delegation designator** `0xef0100 || address` is written into the EOA's code slot.
- Any call to the EOA then runs `address`'s code. The code uses the EOA's own balance and storage.
- The delegation **persists until it is replaced by a new authorization or cleared with `address = 0x0`.**
- The submitter and the signer can differ. In other words, **anyone holding the signature can submit it.**

To follow this incident, you need one part of the processing rule: the nonce check.

```python
# EIP-7702 authorization processing (simplified from the spec)
for auth in tx.authorization_list:
    authority = ecrecover(auth)
    if auth.nonce != get_nonce(authority):
        continue                      # a failed authorization is skipped silently (the tx continues)
    set_code(authority, b"\xef\x01\x00" + auth.address)
    increment_nonce(authority)        # nonce +1 each time one is applied
```

Two properties matter.

1. **A failed authorization does not revert the transaction.** So you can include many authorizations with different nonces. Only one needs to match at execution time.
2. **Each applied authorization bumps the nonce by one.** So the *order* of the authorizations changes the outcome (§5.2).

### 5.2 Fifty authorizations, in descending order

<AuthNonceOrdering locale="en" />

The [delegation transaction](https://etherscan.io/tx/0xad99264d5532ae0dd04b2004eebda05f25657e70dacb764f7ac8d4ccd051298b) carries 50 authorizations. Every one targets the sweeper. Their nonces run consecutively from 18914775 to 18914824.

A Binance hot wallet processes withdrawals nonstop. So its nonce keeps climbing. The attacker obtained authorizations for 50 consecutive nonces and bet that one would match. But the order of the authorizations reveals something more interesting.

- The authorizations were in **descending nonce order** (824 → 775).
- In the same block, at index 133, Binance sent [its own transaction](https://etherscan.io/tx/0xca63641eaeb49779d80ba11ce4fe894d635f2e98c032e9efd5bb4ce304ae56da). Its nonce was 18914808.
- The attack transaction sat at index 138. So the only authorization that applied was **18914809**. After the block, the nonce stood at 18914810.

Why pack them backwards? In ascending order, the nonce would become 810 once 809 applied. Then the next authorization, 810, would match and apply too. The chain would run all the way to 824, a 16-step jump in the nonce. Every withdrawal Binance had already signed (nonces 810–824) would become invalid. That would very likely trip operational alerts at once. In descending order, one authorization applies and every remaining one has a lower nonce. They all fail silently. It is, in effect, **an ordering that minimizes the footprint**.

The gas used, 1,258,562, fits this picture. Start with the 21,000 base cost and add 50 authorizations × 25,000. Subtract a 12,500 refund for the one applied to an existing account, and you land almost exactly there. The 49 failed authorizations still paid gas.

So when were these authorizations signed? Binance 14's nonce passed 18914775 at [20:17:47](https://etherscan.io/block/26300737). That is exactly where the signed range starts. The wallet processes roughly 10 transactions a minute. So 50 nonces buy 4–5 minutes of headroom. The attacker most likely got the authorizations **in real time, about three minutes before submitting them**. That fits TRM's view: "access was time-limited, or signatures had to be requested through the system."

### 5.4 Revocation — Binance never lost its signing ability

The delegation was cleared at 00:10:35 on October 30 by [this transaction](https://etherscan.io/tx/0xafbf94c92a8d749988411669fa14d4f8866d9b4420b4ebd299f28e958dac7209). It was **sent by Binance 14 itself**, with the authorization pointing at `0x0`.

- It is a *self-sponsored* authorization: the sender and the signer are the same account.
- The transaction nonce is 18914903 and the authorization nonce is 18914904. The sender's nonce is incremented before authorizations are processed. So the authorization must use nonce + 1.
- Binance sent 93 transactions from this wallet while it was delegated. The last one was at 00:07:11.
- Binance revoked the delegation with its own key. That means it **never lost its signing ability**. This is consistent with the official statement that no private key leaked.

## 6. The intrusion mechanism — reasoning backward from the evidence

Binance has not disclosed the path from laptop to signature. Still, the evidence narrows the possibilities a lot. Let's start from the typical structure of an exchange hot wallet. Then let's see which paths the evidence allows and which it rules out.

### 6.1 The typical exchange withdrawal pipeline

<WithdrawalPipeline locale="en" />

A large exchange's hot wallet is usually split into these layers.

| Layer | Role | If the attacker controls it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Withdrawal service | Creates user-withdrawal and internal-transfer requests | Can create requests. They still have to pass the policy engine |
| Policy engine | • Address whitelists<br />• Amount limits<br />• Velocity limits<br />• Anomaly detection | Effectively decides what gets signed |
| Signing orchestrator | Converts requests into chain-specific formats and hands them to signers | Reaches every chain at once |
| Per-chain signers | HSM or MPC nodes sign with the real keys | Protects the key. But signs whatever request arrives |
| Ops path | • Deployment via CI/CD<br />• Cluster operations | Can change code or config in any layer above |

[Just like the Secure Enclave](/en/posts/mobile-wallet-key-security) in a phone wallet, a signer never exports its key. Instead, it *signs the requests it receives*. So the attacker's target is not the key. It is **some layer in front of the signer**.

### 6.2 Constraints the evidence imposes

| Observed fact | Basis | Constraint on the mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| • No evidence of key leakage<br />• Binance signed 93 transactions during the delegation<br />• Binance revoked it itself | • Binance<br />• On-chain | The attacker gained **the right to request signatures**, not the keys |
| Wallets on 11 chains with different curves (secp256k1, ed25519) all drained within ~30 minutes | • TRM<br />• Elliptic | Not per-chain signers breached one by one. The attacker reached **a chain-agnostic upper layer** |
| Other chains drained via *validly signed ordinary withdrawals* | • TRM<br />• Elliptic | The attacker used **request formats the signing system normally accepts** |
| The EIP-7702 delegation "did not pass through normal approval controls" | Richard Teng | The policy engine was **bypassed**. Or the request was **in a format it couldn't interpret** |
| • 50 authorizations obtained ~3 minutes before submission<br />• chainId 1 (mainnet only) | On-chain | Not signatures stolen in advance. **Live signing-API access**. Access may have been time-limited |
| • 36 hours of testing and rehearsal<br />• Exact authorization format used | On-chain | The attacker **knew the internal signing API's spec beforehand**. There was a reconnaissance period |
| • "Additional breach points"<br />• Attacker tooling with a "self-destruct mechanism" | Binance | A **multi-stage intrusion**, not a single click. Trace removal slows forensics |

### 6.3 Comparing hypotheses

| Hypothesis | Description | Supporting evidence | Inconsistent or uncertain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **H1. Direct signing-API calls with service credentials** | The compromised container's service-account token or mTLS certificate is used to call the signing orchestrator directly. Possible if the policy engine sits *beside* the signing path, not *in front of* it | • Simultaneous drain on 11 chains<br />• The official account ("withdrawals submitted from a container")<br />• Live access | Should have been blocked if the signer re-checked policy |
| **H2. Abuse of a raw-digest signing path** | The signing API had a path that signs a raw 32-byte hash. A 7702 authorization digest was fed into it (§6.5) | • "7702 didn't pass approval controls"<br />• Other chains used ordinary-withdrawal formats | Whether such an API existed is undisclosed |
| **H3. Tampering with policy-engine settings** | Whitelists and limits changed so withdrawals to attacker addresses pass | Ordinary withdrawals were "validly signed" | • The official wording is *bypassed*, not *passed*<br />• No mention of config changes |
| **H4. Poisoned deployment path (CI/CD)** | Malicious code planted in a signing-related service image or config via DevOps privileges. Same family as the Safe front-end tampering in the Bybit hack | • Entry point was a "DevOps" endpoint<br />• Self-destruct mechanism<br />• Additional breach points | • An image change would show up in deployment records<br />• No mention of one |

The simplest picture that satisfies all these constraints is **H1 + H2**. It goes like this.

1. Obtain cloud and cluster credentials from the DevOps endpoint.
2. Get into a withdrawal-related service container.
3. Use the signing-request rights that container already had.
4. On the other chains, submit requests in ordinary withdrawal format (H1).
5. On Ethereum, get an authorization digest signed that the policy engine cannot interpret (H2).

H4 fits the "DevOps" entry point well enough that it can't be ruled out. H3 fits the official wording least.

### 6.5 Why one authorization signature is more dangerous than thousands of withdrawals

Signing an EIP-7702 authorization means signing *a different message* than a transaction.

```text
EIP-1559 transaction signature:  keccak256(0x02 || rlp([chain_id, nonce, ..., to, value, data, ...]))
EIP-7702 authorization signature: keccak256(0x05 || rlp([chain_id, address, nonce]))
```

To a signer, both are just 32-byte hashes. A policy engine usually checks *decoded transactions*: recipient, amount, calldata. An authorization digest has no recipient and no amount. If the signing API accepts raw hashes, an authorization signature looks to the policy engine **like nothing at all**. That is why H2 fits the official wording ("did not pass through approval controls") so well.

And the authority each signature hands over is in a different league.

| | Withdrawal signature | EIP-7702 authorization signature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scope | • One asset<br />• One amount<br />• Once | • **Every asset** in the wallet<br />• **All future deposits** too |
| Duration | Done once executed | Persists **until revoked** |
| Side privileges | None | Every other contract role the wallet holds (§5.3 `callExecute`) |
| Visibility to the policy engine | Recipient and amount can be checked | No recipient or amount to check |

> **Key insight** — A withdrawal signature is a signature over an *amount*. A delegation signature is a signature over *the account itself*. If signing policy only looks at amounts, the most dangerous signature gets the least scrutiny.

## 7. The freeze race — 37 minutes and 200 million USDT

| Receiving address | USDT blacklist | USDC blacklist | Current balance (frozen) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `0x5227…2f8c` | 21:01:35 | 21:04:59 | • 1,175.46 million USDT<br />• 823 million USDC |
| `0xa97b…8d10` | — | 10-30 04:11:35 | ~120,000 USDC |

- From the first sweep (20:24) to Tether's freeze (21:01) took **37 minutes**. About 644 million USDT got out in that window.
- The 21:44 sweep **sent 200.4 million USDT to an address that was already frozen.** The USDT contract checks the blacklist only for the *sender*. It doesn't block the recipient. So those funds were locked the moment they arrived.
- The attacker didn't switch receiving addresses until 22:39, an hour and 38 minutes after the freeze.

> **Automation failure on both sides** — Binance kept sending deposits into a compromised wallet. The attacker kept sending funds to a frozen address. Both were running *automation that never checked the other side's state*.

Native assets like BTC, XRP, SOL, and NEAR cannot be frozen. None have been. By Elliptic's count, the attacker still holds about \$3.33 billion. A freeze *blocks disposal*. It is not recovery.

## 11. Defensive design — treating signing infrastructure as a security boundary

<SigningPolicyLayers locale="en" />

| Mitigation | Stage it would have stopped here | Trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Signers refuse raw hashes and **decode structured requests themselves** before signing | §6.5 authorization-digest bypass (H2) | A parser per chain and message format |
| Policy checked **again** at the signer (policy engine placed in series, in front of the signing path) | §6.3 direct calls with service credentials (H1) | • Latency<br />• Policy maintained twice |
| Signing-request rights split per service and per chain, with short-lived credentials | §6.3 one container reaching 11 chains | Credential-issuance infrastructure |
| Default-deny EIP-7702 authorizations in signing policy | §5.2 the delegation itself | Legitimate delegations (e.g., AA migration) need a separate approval flow |
| Treat a batch request for authorizations across many nonces as an indicator of compromise | §5.2 the 50-authorization batch | Almost never seen in normal ops, so cheap |
| • Real-time monitoring of treasury delegation state<br />• A pre-tested revocation runbook | §5.3 delegation lasting 3h49m → minutes | Monitoring infrastructure |
| Automatically halt deposit consolidation when a delegation is detected | §5.3 the 23:56 sweep | Deposit delays on false positives |
| Separate signing infrastructure by chain and wallet tier | §3 simultaneous drain on 11 chains | Operational complexity |
| • Separate operator endpoints from everyday work<br />• Separate approval for privilege escalation | §1 initial access | Developer productivity |

Monitoring for delegation is simpler than it sounds. Just watch for an EOA that should have no code suddenly carrying a delegation designator.

```ts
// EIP-7702 delegation monitor for treasury EOAs (concept code)
const DELEGATION_PREFIX = "0xef0100"; // EIP-7702 delegation designator prefix
const POLL_INTERVAL_MS = 12_000;      // check once per Ethereum block (~12 s)

async function watchDelegation(eoa: `0x${string}`, alert: (target: string) => void) {
  for (;;) {
    const code = await client.getCode({ address: eoa });
    if (code?.startsWith(DELEGATION_PREFIX)) {
      alert(`0x${code.slice(DELEGATION_PREFIX.length)}`); // delegation target address
    }
    await new Promise((r) => setTimeout(r, POLL_INTERVAL_MS));
  }
}
```

In production, there is a faster way than polling. Recover the signers of `authorizationList` entries in each new block's type-4 transactions and match them against your addresses. That flags an authorization the moment it lands in a block.

> **Practical recommendation** — "Who can request a signature" matters more than "where the key lives." The signing policy engine is as important a security boundary as the key-custody hardware. Signatures that *change the nature of the account*, like EIP-7702, belong in a different tier from ordinary withdrawals.

## 12. What's established and what remains

What's established is clear. The attacker went through a DevOps engineer's laptop into an internal service container. From there, they submitted unauthorized withdrawals. Binance did not lose its private keys. On Ethereum, EIP-7702 authorizations spanning 50 nonces delegated Binance 14 to a sweeper. Binance revoked the delegation with its own key 3 hours and 49 minutes later.

The open questions are just as clear. How was the laptop infected? What were the additional breach points? Through what path did the signing requests bypass the policy engine? Was the signing system an HSM or MPC? Is the culprit really TraderTraitor? Binance's post-mortem needs to answer these. Above all, it should show which hypothesis in §6 was right.

## 13. Conclusion

> Binance didn't lose its keys. It lost *the authority to decide what gets signed*.

- **The keys didn't leak, but the funds were stolen.** The signing-delegation paradox happened at exchange scale.
- **The security boundary must extend from the key to every signing-request path.** The whole chain — laptop → container → signing system — belongs in the threat model.
- **A withdrawal signature is a signature over an amount; a delegation signature is a signature over the account.** If signing policy only looks at amounts, the most dangerous signature gets the least scrutiny.
- **Response speed sets the size of the loss.** Tether took 37 minutes to freeze. Revocation took 3 hours 49 minutes. Deposits kept flowing in after withdrawals were halted.

So was EIP-7702 the cause? The other 10 chains were drained without it. On Ethereum, though, a single delegation signature handed over the entire wallet. That included every deposit still to come. That is why the same intrusion grew into a 3-hour-49-minute *account takeover*.

In the end, the question isn't "is EIP-7702 dangerous?" It is **"does our signing system know how much a signature weighs?"** A signature that moves an amount and one that hands over an account look like the same 32-byte hash. But they can never sit in the same tier. Security extends to knowing what you're signing.
